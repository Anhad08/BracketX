Volume Two The Operating Experience

Behaviour

Volume One decided what Streamatrix is. Volume Two decides what it does — where panels go, what Escape means, what happens when the GPU dies at 20:14, and how an operator on hour eleven knows the take landed without having to look for proof.

The test for every clause here is the same: could a new engineer implement it without asking a question, and could a reviewer fail a build against it? Anything that survives only as taste has been cut.

01

Preamble

standing orders

Volume One's seven laws are the constitution and are not restated here. Volume Two adds no law and amends none. Where a clause below appears to conflict with a law, the law wins and this document is defective — file it as such rather than reconciling it locally.

Two of those laws do most of the work in this volume and are worth having in front of you:

Law 1 · carried forward Meaning never waits for motion. Any change with broadcast consequence is committed on the input frame.
Law 7 · carried forward Every surface answers “what now?”. There is no dead state.

One addition of scope, not of law: Volume Two is written for the operator, not the author. Where authoring convenience and operating safety conflict, operating safety wins, and the authoring cost is documented rather than argued away. A designer who is mildly inconvenienced at 14:00 is a fair price for a director who cannot make a mistake at 20:00.

02

Workspace Architecture

drag a panel

The workspace is the system that most determines whether Streamatrix reads as an instrument or as a web application in a dark theme. Three products define the space we are refusing, and it is worth being explicit about each, because "not like Electron" is a mood and not a specification.

What we refuse, and why

  • VS Code — panels tab-stack. A tab hides a panel's state behind another panel's, and a hidden panel on a live desk is a panel you forgot about. Streamatrix never tabs a panel.
  • Figma — the canvas is infinite and the chrome floats over it. Broadcast output is a fixed raster; an infinite canvas is a lie about the medium, and floating chrome occludes the frame.
  • Adobe — every panel is equal and everything can go anywhere. Freedom with no structure means every operator's desk is different, which destroys the one thing a gallery needs: another person can sit down and work.

What replaces it

  • One fixed centre. The stage is not a panel and cannot be moved, closed, tabbed or floated.
  • Three docks, orbiting. Left, right, bottom. A panel is in one of them, on a second monitor, or collapsed — there is no fourth condition.
  • Collapse, never hide. A collapsed panel keeps a 17 px spine carrying its name and lamp. You can always see everything you have.
  • Layouts belong to a role. Not to a person, and not to a machine.
The centre rule The stage is not a panel. It cannot be docked, floated, closed, resized below its minimum, or moved to another monitor. Everything else in Streamatrix orbits it. This single constraint is what makes the workspace configurable without becoming unrecognisable — an operator sitting down at any Streamatrix desk in the world knows where the picture is before they look.

Docking doctrine

W1
A panel is in exactly one of four conditions. Docked to left, right or bottom; assigned to a secondary monitor; collapsed to its spine; or removed from the layout entirely. There is no floating condition on the primary display — see W4.
W2
Panels never tab-stack. Two panels in a dock split it; they do not hide each other. Why: a tab makes the cost of a mistake invisible — you cannot see that the Outputs panel is showing an amber lamp if the Assets panel is in front of it. The maximum panels per dock is three, and the fourth drop is refused with a reason rather than accepted and squeezed.
W3
Drop zones are docks, not pixels. There is no free geometry. You drop into left, not into a 312 px column at x=44. Why: free geometry means every layout is unique, unshareable and unrecoverable. Sizes are adjusted afterwards by dragging a seam, and are clamped to each panel's declared minimum.
W4
Nothing floats over the primary display. No floating panels, no palettes, no detached inspectors. Why: the primary display carries the program picture, and anything over it is occluding the show. The only overlays permitted are the command palette and a modal dialog — both of which are forbidden while on air (see §04).
W5
Collapse keeps the spine; nothing is ever fully hidden. A collapsed panel is a 17 px strip with its name and its lamp. Why: an operator must be able to answer "what is in this workspace?" by looking, not by remembering. A panel that can vanish is a panel that can be forgotten while it is warning you.
W6
Auto-hide does not exist. No panel slides away on blur and returns on hover. Why: it makes layout a function of pointer position, so the same screen looks different at different moments and muscle memory cannot form. Space is bought by collapsing deliberately, which is a decision the operator made and can see.
W7
Pinning protects, it does not position. A pinned panel cannot be moved, collapsed or replaced by a preset — including by someone else's preset. Why: during a show an operator needs to know their Outputs panel will still be there in twenty minutes. Pin is the only setting that survives a preset change.
W8
Layouts are bound to a role and a form factor, never to a person. Director × truck and Director × laptop are two stored geometries of one layout. Why: the operator changes seat more often than they change job. Sitting at a different desk must not mean rebuilding your workspace, and covering someone's shift must not mean inheriting their idiosyncrasies.

The cost we accept

W3 and W4 together mean a designer cannot pull the Inspector out onto the desktop and park it wherever they like, which is a real convenience in authoring tools and one that authoring users will ask for. We are refusing it on operating grounds and stating the cost rather than pretending it is not one: a second monitor is the sanctioned answer, and if a user has only one screen the answer is collapse, not float.

Multi-monitor

G1
The primary display holds the stage and never anything else's primary job. Monitors are assigned by role — Program, Work, Monitoring — not by index. Why: display indices change when someone unplugs a cable; roles do not. On reconnection, roles are reassigned to whatever hardware is present and the operator is told which display took which job.
G2
A lost monitor never loses a panel. Panels assigned to a display that disappears return to their home dock on the primary, collapsed, with an amber lamp. Why: the alternative — a panel that exists but is nowhere — is the single most common multi-monitor failure in professional software, and it is unrecoverable without a settings reset.
G3
A full-screen program output is not a window. It has no chrome, no cursor after 2 s, no notifications, no screensaver, and cannot be focused by Tab. Why: it is frequently pointed at a camera or a wall in a studio. Anything drawn on it is on television.
04

Persistence & Recovery

what survives

Workspace state is written continuously to a journal, not saved. There is no "save workspace" command because there is no moment at which an operator would think to issue one, and a preference that can be lost is a preference that will be lost — usually by the crash that made you want it back.

What is restored after a crashand what deliberately is not
RestoredReasoning
Panel geometryyesThe layout is the operator's tool. Losing it is losing their hands.
SelectionyesRestoring geometry without selection puts you back in the room but not back at work.
Scroll & timeline positionyesCheap to store, expensive to re-find under pressure.
Undo historyyesA crash is not a decision. The stack survives it, capped at the session.
Rundown & cue pointsyesThe show's script. Never derived, always journalled.
Preview / cued graphicre-armed, not re-cuedThe graphic is selected and ready; the operator presses cue. A machine that comes back armed is a machine that made a broadcast decision on its own.
On-air stateneverStreamatrix always restarts off air, and says so in plain words. Nothing goes to air except by a person.
Recovery is not a dialog. The workspace is simply there when it reopens. The only message is the on-air one, because that is the only thing that changed against expectation.
The restart rule Streamatrix never returns to air by itself. Not after a crash, not after an update, not after a power cut, not after a network partition heals. Automatic recovery of output is a feature that ships graphics to a live audience with no human in the loop, and no amount of convenience justifies it.

Presets & reset

P1
Four presets ship, and they are named after jobs. Designer, Director, Operator, Engineer. Why: a preset named "Layout 2" carries no information and is never chosen. A preset named after a job tells a new hire which one is theirs on their first day.
P2
A preset is applied, not entered. Applying a preset changes the layout and leaves you free to modify it; you are not "in" a mode you must leave. Modifications mark the layout modified and the preset name persists as its origin. Why: modes that must be exited are forgotten and then blamed.
P3
Reset restores the preset, not the factory. Reset returns you to the preset you started from, one press, no confirmation, undoable. A factory reset is a separate, deliberate command in Settings. Why: the thing an operator wants when the layout is a mess is "put it back how it was this morning".
P4
Layout changes are never undoable by ⌘Z. Undo belongs to the document; layout is not the document. Layout is reverted by Reset. Why: the single most common undo accident in creative software is pressing ⌘Z after moving a panel and destroying twenty minutes of work.

Windows & Modality

the layer model

Five layers, strictly ordered. A thing exists at exactly one layer, and a layer's rules are absolute — which is what makes Escape predictable, and predictability is the whole point.

Sheets and drawers do not exist as separate concepts. They are layer 1 or 2 with different geometry. Inventing a sixth layer to hold them would make Escape ambiguous.
The modal rule No modal dialog may open while on air. Not for confirmation, not for a licence check, not for an update, not for an error. A modal on air blocks the operator from the show to talk about something less important than the show — by definition, since nothing is more important than the show. Anything that would have been a modal becomes a notification with the same actions.
The Escape ladderpress Escape · it peels exactly one layer
Never does Escape leave the production, close the window, or stop the show. The ladder bottoms out at "nothing selected" and stops there.

Confirmation doctrine

Confirmations are a tax on every future use of a command, paid to prevent a rare mistake. Streamatrix charges that tax only where the mistake is irreversible and unforeseeable — both, not either.

Which guard appliesdecided by reversibility, not by fear
CaseGuardExample
ReversibleNone. Ever.Cue, take, layout change, remove from rundown, hide a layer.
Reversible, expensiveNone, plus an undo affordance in the result notification.Deleting a layer with children — "Deleted 12 layers · Undo".
Irreversible, foreseeableDanger styling only. The button says what happens."Discard recording" — you opened the recording to discard it.
Irreversible, unforeseeableModal, stating the consequence in the first sentence, off air only.Deleting a production that another operator has open.
Irreversible, on airRefused. The command is unavailable, with the reason on the control.Deleting the graphic currently on Program.
Never a "don't ask me again" checkbox. If it is safe to skip, it should not have been asked; if it is not, the checkbox is a trap set for the operator's future self.

Type-to-confirm is banned

  • Typing a production's name to delete it trains the hand, not the mind — after the third time it is muscle memory and guards nothing.
  • It cannot be done under time pressure, so it becomes the thing an operator does quickly and wrongly.
  • Where the stake is genuinely that high, the correct design is to make the action reversible for 30 seconds, not to make it harder to perform.

The 30-second window

  • Destructive actions off air are performed immediately and held reversible for 30 s.
  • The notification carries the undo, and it is the only notification that does not expire early.
  • The work is genuinely destroyed at 30 s, not archived silently — a promise of deletion is kept.
06

Keyboard

philosophy, then bindings

A director does not use a mouse during a show. The keyboard is the instrument and the pointer is the workshop, so the bindings are organised by where the hand lives rather than by which menu the command appears in. Six rules govern the whole map, and every binding below is derivable from them.

K1
Unmodified keys belong to live operation. Authoring always takes a modifier. Why: during a show a stray keypress must never edit the document. D cannot mean "duplicate" because a hand resting on the keyboard would duplicate a layer on air. Live commands are single keys precisely because they must be reachable without looking.
K2
A binding may change meaning only across the on-air boundary. Not by panel, not by focus, not by tool. Why: context-dependent bindings are the reason professional users distrust shortcuts. On-air is the single piece of context an operator is never uncertain about — it is a red bar across the top of the room.
K3
Modifiers have fixed meanings, product-wide. ⌘ commit or system · ⇧ extend or reverse · ⌥ precision and reveal · ⌃ is left to the operating system and never bound. Why: a user who learns four modifiers can predict a binding they have never used.
K4
No chords, no leader keys, no sequences. Every command is one keystroke with modifiers. Why: a sequence has a halfway state, and a halfway state under pressure is a machine that is listening for something you have forgotten you started. There is no Streamatrix equivalent of ⌘K ⌘S.
K5
Danger is inversely proportional to reach — except panic. Destructive bindings require two modifiers and sit away from the home position. The one exception is the panic key, which must be hittable while looking at a monitor, because the situation it exists for is one where you have three seconds.
K6
Every binding is printed on its control. As a keycap, on the button, always visible — never only in a help sheet or a tooltip. Why: shortcuts are learned by peripheral repetition, not by study. A binding nobody discovers is a binding that does not exist.

The map, by hand position

The one binding to defend ⏎ takes to air, unconditionally, while on air — including when a text field has focus. The field commits on blur instead. A show outranks a form, and an operator who has to think about where focus is before they can cut has been given a machine that is thinking about itself.

Pointer

13 · hover each

Volume One drew eight. Five more complete the set — the authoring cursors and the two commerce cursors — and the rules are unchanged: 24 px, monochrome, one-pixel dark keyline so it survives on a white title card and a black well, no drop shadow, exact hotspot.

The governing rule is worth restating because it is violated constantly in software: the cursor changes on capability, not on hover styling. If the cursor changes, a click must do something. A pointer cursor over non-interactive text is a promise the product then breaks.

The Broadcast State Model

click a state

Twelve operational states. Every one is defined across eight dimensions, so that no engineer ever has to decide what a lamp should do — and so that no two features answer the same question differently.

Two distinctions do a great deal of work and are frequently collapsed by other products, to their cost. Program is not Live: Program means a graphic is on the local program bus; Live means that bus is reaching an audience. A rehearsal is Program without Live, and an operator must be able to see the difference or they will rehearse on television. Recording is orthogonal: it can be true in any state, which is why it is a separate lamp and never a colour on the spine.

09

Confidence

the receipt doctrine

Every question an operator asks themselves is a failure of the interface. Did it save? Did it take? Did it install? — each is half a second of doubt, and half a second repeated four hundred times in a twelve-hour shift is the difference between a tool that is trusted and one that is merely used.

The receipt doctrine Every consequential action leaves evidence, in the place the operator is already looking, within 200 ms, without being asked, and without requiring an acknowledgement. Evidence is a state change on the object itself — never a toast that says the thing was done and then removes the proof.
Six doubts, answeredwhere the evidence lives
DoubtThe answer, permanently visibleWhy there
Did it take?The program monitor shows it, the tally spine is red, and the on-air clock is counting. Three independent signals.The most consequential action gets redundancy, because a single failed indicator must not be able to lie.
Did it save?Nothing is saved, so nothing can fail to be. The document is journalled continuously; the title bar shows the last written moment, not a dirty dot.A dirty-state indicator asks the operator to manage persistence. That is the machine's job.
Did it install?The package appears in the library with an owned mark, and the library count increments on the same frame.Evidence at the destination, not at the source. You look where the thing now lives.
Did it publish?The output row shows the destination, its lamp, its bitrate and its uptime. Live is a readout, never a past-tense message.Publishing is a continuing state, so a moment-in-time confirmation would be a lie one second later.
Did it update?The version on the package changes. If a graphic changed visually, it is flagged for review before it can be used on air.An update that silently changes what goes to television is the worst class of surprise.
Is it still there?Every dependency the show needs is verified on open and continuously thereafter; the rundown shows a lamp per item.A missing font is discovered at 20:00 by looking at it, or at 19:00 by the product. We choose 19:00.
Corollary Streamatrix has no "Saved!", no "Success!", no green tick that fades. Success is silent and visible; only failure speaks.

Failure

12 classes

Every failure message answers four questions in this order, and the order is not negotiable because it is the order an operator needs them under pressure: What happened? What is affected? Can the show continue? What should I do? The third is the one almost all software omits, and it is the one that determines whether the operator panics.

Failure classesseverity is defined by effect on air, never by technical seriousness
ClassSeverity ruleBehaviourShow continues?
OutputLive if on air, else attention.Red spine, program lamp red, one action: restart or switch. The only failure permitted to use live red.Only if a second output exists. Stated either way.
RenderingAttention.The frame that failed is held, never blanked. Black is worse than stale on television.Yes, degraded and visibly labelled.
EngineAttention, escalating.The engine is restarted in place with scene state re-projected. The operator is told it happened, after it has succeeded.Yes, with a ~400 ms gap on the affected surface.
GPUAttention.Falls back to the software path at reduced resolution and says so explicitly. Never a crash, never a blank window.Yes, degraded. The degradation is named on the output row.
HardwareAttention.Capture, audio and control-surface loss are reported per device, with the last-good timestamp.Depends on the device, and the message says which.
NetworkQuiet unless output is affected.Offline is a working state, not an error. Everything local continues; changes queue.Yes. Always, for local production.
MarketplaceQuiet.Never interrupts. Failures surface only when the operator next goes there.Yes. A shop being down is not an incident.
LicenceQuiet, never blocking.A lapsed licence never stops a running show, ever. It warns off air, and warns earlier each day.Yes, unconditionally.
StorageAttention, early.Warns at the point where the current recording would not fit — a time figure, not a percentage.Yes, until it isn't; the warning is calibrated to arrive with hours to spare.

Never a technical cause in the first sentence. Always one behind “Show detail”, complete and copyable. Never the word “error”, “failed”, “invalid”, “oops”, or an exclamation mark.
The honesty rule Streamatrix never hides a failure to protect the mood, and never dramatises one to seem thorough. If the product does not know whether the show is affected, it says “I don't yet know whether Output 1 is affected — checking” and then answers. An operator can work with uncertainty. They cannot work with false confidence.

The Invisible Product

15 systems

The systems nobody praises and everybody notices when they are wrong. These are where the engineering excellence in Volume One's mission statement actually lives — not in a material, but in the fact that the product never loses anything.

Human Factors

borrowed principles

Four disciplines have solved parts of this problem under stakes higher than ours. We borrow their principles and refuse their visuals — a broadcast application that looks like a cockpit is cosplay, and cosplay is the opposite of what these fields teach.

Designing for hour eleven

Fatigue does not make people slower in a way that scales evenly. It makes them lose state — they forget which mode they are in, which item is selected, whether they already did the thing. Every decision below is aimed at that specific failure, not at general "usability".

H1
No invisible modes.If a mode exists, the cursor, a lamp and the status line all say so. Fatigue's first casualty is the memory of having entered something.
H2
Nothing moves unless a person moved it.No auto-scroll, no auto-select, no reordering under the pointer, no list that re-sorts when data arrives. A tired operator clicking where a thing was two seconds ago must hit the thing.
H3
The same action always occupies the same pixels.Take is in the same place in every production, at every window size, in every preset. Muscle memory is only earned by absolute positional stability.
H4
Destructive and constructive controls are never adjacent.Minimum 24 px and, where possible, a different material between them. Under fatigue, adjacency is the mechanism of the accident.
H5
Low light is the design target, not a theme.The dark finish is the reference; Light Studio is the departure. Peak luminance is capped so that a white dialog cannot dazzle someone who has been dark-adapted for four hours.
H6
Nothing is conveyed by hue alone.Every semantic colour is paired with a shape, a position or a word. Eight per cent of male operators cannot rely on red-versus-green, and every one of them works in this industry.
H7
Readouts never flicker.Numbers update on a fixed cadence with tabular figures and fixed widths. A value that jitters is read as instability in the system it measures.

Conformance

C11 – C22

Volume One's checks C1–C10 stand. These extend them to behaviour. As before, each is phrased so a reviewer who did not design the feature can apply it and fail a build.

C11
The stage never moves.Attempt to dock, float, close or tab it. Every route must be absent — not disabled.
C12
No panel can be lost.Assign every panel to a second monitor, then disconnect it. All must return, collapsed, with an amber lamp.
C13
Escape peels exactly one layer, every time.Build the full stack and press Escape five times. It must never skip a layer or leave the production.
C14
No modal exists while on air.Force every error class with the show live. Each must arrive as a notification carrying the same actions.
C15
Restart is always off air.Kill the process while live, in every state. It must return off air and say so.
C16
⌘Z never changes the layout.Move a panel, then undo. The panel must stay where it was put.
C17
Every live command is reachable without the pointer.Unplug the mouse and run a complete show, including recovery from a failed output.
C18
Every failure answers all four questions.Read each message. If "can the show continue?" is unanswered, it fails — regardless of how well written it is.
C19
