Volume FourInvention

Things that should already exist

Volumes One to Three decided how Streamatrix behaves. This one decides what it does that nothing else does — and the test is uncomfortable on purpose: after six months, would a broadcaster going back to their old system notice this was gone, and be angry about it?

Everything here is buildable against the engine as it exists or as a stated extension of it. Nothing below requires a research breakthrough, and nothing below is a feature nobody asked for — each one exists because broadcast graphics fail in a specific, boring, repeated way that the industry has collectively decided to live with.

00

Doctrine of Invention

what earns a place

The failure mode of a product like this is not too few features. It is a hundred good ideas that make the instrument heavier. Five rules decide what gets built.

I1
Invent against a failure, not against a competitor. Every invention here starts from a specific way broadcast graphics go wrong on air — a name that overflowed, a font that was missing, a feed that changed shape at 19:00. Features derived from a competitor's roadmap arrive without a reason to exist and cannot be argued about.
I2
Move work earlier, not faster. The most valuable thing software can do for a broadcaster is convert a 20:00 problem into an 18:00 problem. Nine of the twelve inventions below are earliness machines. Speed at 20:00 is worth far less than knowledge at 18:00.
I3
The product may know things. It may not decide things. Analysis, prediction, verification and warning are all legitimate. Acting on the operator's behalf on the live path is not, ever — including when the product is right. See §12.
I4
Nothing new may appear during a show. An invention that is only visible on air has failed: it is asking the operator to learn something at the worst possible moment. Everything here is discovered off air and merely trusted on air.
I5
If it cannot be turned off, it is not finished. Every invention below can be disabled per production, and the product must remain coherent without it. A feature that becomes load-bearing without consent is a feature that cannot be trusted.
00b

What We Will Not Build

stated first, on purpose

Stated before the inventions rather than after, because a list of refusals written after the fact is a rationalisation. These are the things a competitor will ship, that we will be asked for, and that we will decline.

Never

  • Anything that puts content on air without a person. No auto-take, no scheduled take, no “AI director”, no automatic recovery to air. This is the line the whole product is organised around.
  • Generative content on the live path. A model may propose; a human must have seen the rendered result before it can reach an audience.
  • Engagement metrics. No “you used Streamatrix for 6 hours today”. A tool is not a habit, and time-in-app is an anti-goal for production software.
  • Gamification. No streaks, badges, levels or achievement toasts. Broadcasting is already high-stakes; adding fake stakes is contemptuous.
  • Subjective marketplace ratings. Five stars measure popularity, not fitness. See Amendment 3 for what replaces them.
  • A plugin API into the live render path. Third-party code between the operator and the audience is a supply chain we cannot certify. Extensions live outside the frame path or not at all.
  • Cloud-only capability. Nothing an operator needs during a show may require the internet. Ever.

Because

  • Every one of these is popular, defensible in a pitch, and would make the product measurably worse at the only thing it is for.
  • A broadcaster's trust is built over years and lost in one incident. Features that trade a small convenience for a small probability of catastrophe are always a bad trade at this scale.
  • The refusals are also the marketing. “Streamatrix will never put something on air by itself” is a sentence a head of broadcast can repeat to their board.
01

The Dry Run

a compiler for a broadcast
01

Execute the entire rundown at 200× before anyone goes near the desk

Rundown intelligenceBroadcast safetyDiagnostics
A rundown is a program. Streamatrix runs it — every graphic, every transition, every data binding, against the real engine and the real feeds — and reports what would have gone wrong, in about four seconds, before the studio is even lit.
Why does this exist?
Because a rundown is already a deterministic sequence of state changes, and we have an engine that can produce them faster than real time. The information is free; nobody collects it.
Why has nobody done it?
In most systems the rundown lives in a newsroom computer, the graphics live in a renderer, and the data lives in a third place. No single component can execute the show. Streamatrix's document model means one process holds all three.
What problem does it solve?
The commonest broadcast graphics failure is not a crash. It is a graphic that was never rendered with today's data until the moment it went to air — a missing font, a null binding, a substituted asset, an animation that collides with the one before it.
What trade-off?
It costs GPU time, it can be wrong about live feeds that change after the run, and it creates a false sense of safety if operators treat a green run as a guarantee. The report therefore states what it could not check as prominently as what it did.
What doctrine?
I2 — move work earlier. Also Volume Two's pre-air check, of which this is the executable half.
Ten years out
This becomes the thing nobody remembers living without, in the way that nobody now writes code without a type checker. “You went to air without running it?”
Would a broadcaster miss it after six months? Yes — this is the one they would refuse to give up.

Worst Case

playable
02

Design against the longest name in the feed, not the one in the mock-up

Graphics authoringTemplate systemsBroadcast safety
Every graphic is continuously rendered against the adversarial extreme of its own data source — the longest name, the largest number, the widest string, the most rows — and shows you the failure while you are still designing it.
Specimena real lower third against a real squad list
Amara Okonkwo
Chief Correspondent · Lagos
Fits
This is the value the designer typed. It says nothing about the other 23 in the feed.
The point The middle state is what goes to air today, in every broadcast graphics system in the world, and nobody finds out until it happens.
Why does this exist?
Because the single most visible on-air graphics failure — text spilling out of a box — is entirely predictable from data the product already has.
Why has nobody done it?
Because template systems treat data as something that arrives at runtime. Streamatrix's variables declare their source, so the extremes are knowable at design time. It is a consequence of the data model, not a feature bolted on.
What problem does it solve?
A designer who has only ever seen “Amara Okonkwo” has not designed a graphic. They have designed one instance of it. Worst Case makes the difference visible in the moment it matters.
What trade-off?
Designing for the extreme can make the typical case look timid. The answer is auto-fitting with a declared floor — and the floor is a legibility limit, not an aesthetic one, so it can be enforced by Continuous Legal (§05).
What doctrine?
I2, and Volume One's Law 7 — the surface answers “what now?” by offering the fit rather than only reporting the overflow.
Ten years out
Designing a broadcast graphic without seeing its worst case looks like shipping code without running the tests.
Would a broadcaster miss it after six months? Yes, and specifically the first time a name overflowed on air somewhere else.
03

Frame Budget

playable
03

Not a frame rate. A budget, spent per layer, known before air

TelemetryRenderingVisual debugging
Every layer declares its cost in milliseconds. The operator sees the show's frame budget the way an engineer sees a power budget — attributed, in advance, with headroom stated — instead of discovering it as a dropped frame on television.
SpecimenMatch Day rundown · 1080p50 · 20.0 ms per frame
7.1 ms of headroom
The heaviest frame in this rundown costs 12.9 ms. Safe at 1080p50.
Attribution is the invention. Every renderer can tell you it dropped a frame. None of them tells you which layer to blame, before you find out on air.
Why does this exist?
Because “it ran fine on my machine at 1080” is how graphics teams discover, live, that a show does not fit at 2160p50.
Why has nobody done it?
Because renderers measure frames, not layers. Streamatrix's projection is per-node, so a per-node cost is already in the pipeline — it just has to be surfaced as an operator instrument instead of a developer profiler.
What problem does it solve?
It converts the least-diagnosable class of broadcast failure — intermittent stutter under load — into a number attached to the object that caused it.
What trade-off?
Measurement costs a little of what it measures, and worst-case cost is not the same as typical cost. The instrument therefore shows the heaviest frame in the rundown, not the average — pessimism is the correct bias here.
What doctrine?
I2 and Volume Two's GPU doctrine — degradation must be predicted and named, never discovered.
Ten years out
A graphics package ships with its cost on the box, the way a component ships with a power draw. The Marketplace lists it. See Amendment 3.
Would a broadcaster miss it after six months? The engineers would. The director would miss the absence of stutter.
04

The Data Contract

a type system for a show
04

A graphic declares what it needs. The feed is validated against it, continuously

Template systemsDiagnosticsBroadcast safety
score: integer 0–99, required · name: text ≤ 24 glyphs, required · badge: image 1:1, optional, falls back to competition mark. When the feed stops satisfying the contract, Streamatrix knows at 18:04, names the field, and shows you which graphics are affected.
Why does this exist?
Because a live data feed is an untyped interface owned by someone else, consumed at the worst possible moment, and it changes without notice.
Why has nobody done it?
Broadcast graphics historically bind by name and hope. The industry's answer to a changed feed is a phone call. Nobody has treated the feed as an interface with a contract because nobody modelled graphics as programs.
What problem does it solve?
The 19:00 discovery that the scores endpoint now returns strings, or that a name field gained a middle initial and every lower third is now three glyphs too wide.
What trade-off?
It is work at design time, and designers will resent it exactly as much as engineers resent types — until the first time it saves them. Contracts are therefore inferred from the first sample and then confirmed, never authored from scratch.
What doctrine?
I2, and it is the precondition for both Worst Case and The Understudy — neither works without knowing what the data promises.
Ten years out
Data providers publish Streamatrix contracts alongside their APIs, and a feed change ships as a versioned contract change. This is the invention with the most leverage on the wider industry.
Would a broadcaster miss it after six months?Not consciously — they would just stop having a category of bad night.
05

Continuous Legal

compliance as a live instrument
05

Gamut, luma, safe area, minimum legible type, and flash rate — checked as you design

Broadcast safetyRenderingApproval
Broadcast has actual legal limits — colour gamut, peak luminance, and in several territories a statutory limit on flashing imagery for photosensitive epilepsy. Streamatrix checks all of them continuously, on the graphic, while it is being made, and refuses to let an unchecked graphic be marked ready.
Why does this exist?
Because these are the only requirements in the product that carry a regulator, and they are currently enforced by a person watching a scope after the fact — if at all.
Why has nobody done it?
Compliance tooling lives in transmission, at the end of the chain, as a separate box. Putting it in the authoring tool means catching it at the point where fixing it is free.
What problem does it solve?
Illegal colour that has to be legalised in transmission and comes out looking wrong; type that is unreadable at delivery resolution; a transition that strobes at 4 Hz and breaches Ofcom guidance.
What trade-off?
It constrains designers, sometimes visibly — a brand red may not be legal. The product's job is to say so and offer the nearest legal colour, not to silently clamp it, which is what transmission does today.
What doctrine?
I2, and Volume Three's accessibility rule against photosensitive triggers — extended from the interface to the output.
Ten years out
A graphics tool that cannot prove its output is legal is not usable by a public broadcaster. This becomes a procurement requirement.
Would a broadcaster miss it after six months?Their compliance officer would refuse to sign off without it.
06

The Understudy

designed degradation
06

Every graphic declares what it becomes when something is missing

Broadcast safetyTemplate systemsOperator assistance
A graphic is not one design. It is a design plus its declared degradations: what it looks like with no badge, with no photo, with a null score, with the font unavailable. Authored deliberately, verified by the Dry Run, and used automatically on air — because a graphic that is slightly wrong beats a hole in the programme.
Why does this exist?
Because the alternative to degradation is absence, and on television absence is the worse failure. Black is always worse than stale.
Why has nobody done it?
Because fallbacks are usually a rendering concern — a substituted font, an empty box — decided by the engine at the moment of failure. Making the fallback an authored, reviewable part of the design is the shift.
What problem does it solve?
The lower third that goes to air with a grey rectangle where a badge should be, because nobody ever decided what should happen instead.
What trade-off?
More design work per graphic, and a risk that a degraded form goes out and nobody notices it was degraded. Mitigated by a rule: whenever an understudy is on air, the operator is told, on the object, in amber.
What doctrine?
Volume Two's rendering failure class — “the frame that failed is held, never blanked” — generalised from frames to content. It is also the answer to anyone arguing for automatic recovery to air: degrade, do not resurrect.
Ten years out
Shipping a broadcast template without its understudies looks like shipping a web page with no error state.
Would a broadcaster miss it after six months?Yes — and it is the one their competitors would notice on air.

Critical Path

readiness, as a countdown
07

The rundown knows how long each item takes to become ready — and warns before you get there

Rundown intelligenceOperator assistanceTelemetry
Every graphic has a real readiness latency: fetch, decode, upload, first frame. Streamatrix measures it, and the rundown shows “item 14 needs 2.1 s and you are 1.4 s away” — as a state on the item, before the operator arrives at it, not as a stall when they press take.
Why does this exist?
Because “I pressed take and nothing happened” is almost always a readiness problem, and it is invisible until it bites.
Why has nobody done it?
Systems pre-load a fixed lookahead of one or two items and hope. Nobody measures actual per-item readiness and compares it to the operator's actual position and pace in the rundown.
What problem does it solve?
A fast-paced show — esports, news breaking, a goal — where the operator outruns the machine and finds out by taking a graphic that is not there.
What trade-off?
It means predicting where the operator is going, which is a guess. The mitigation is that it only ever warms and warns — it never reorders, never skips, and never takes. And what it has warmed is visible, so there is no hidden state.
What doctrine?
I3 — the product may know, it may not decide. And Volume Two's H2: nothing moves unless a person moved it.
Ten years out
Readiness becomes as normal a readout as timecode, and a rundown without it feels like driving without a fuel gauge.
Would a broadcaster miss it after six months?Every operator who has ever taken an empty graphic, immediately.
08

Second Pair of Eyes

two-person integrity
08

Some graphics cannot go to air until a second person has seen them rendered

Approval systemsReview toolsMulti-operator
Not a workflow tool. A broadcast-safety mechanic, borrowed from nuclear two-key and aviation cross-check: a graphic marked for verification is unavailable to the take control until a second identity has viewed the rendered output with the actual data in it — not a filename, not a thumbnail, not a description.
Why does this exist?
Because the highest-consequence graphics errors — a wrong name on an obituary, a wrong sponsor, a wrong election number — are approved by someone reading a list, not seeing a picture.
Why has nobody done it?
Approval in broadcast is a human process outside the software, so the software has no way to enforce that the approver actually looked at the thing. Streamatrix can, because it renders and it knows who is looking.
What problem does it solve?
Approval theatre. A sign-off that attests to a name in a rundown rather than to pixels on a screen.
What trade-off?
It costs time and a second person, so it must be opt-in per graphic, never per production — a blanket policy would be routed around within a week, which is how safety mechanisms die.
What doctrine?
Volume Two's mission-control principle: one console owns one system and can call a hold. Also Volume One Law 6 — the take control is genuinely unavailable, not disabled-with-a-tooltip.
Ten years out
Regulated content — election results, financial data, obituaries — is legally required to carry a verification record. Streamatrix already produces one.
Would a broadcaster miss it after six months?A newsroom would. A sports producer would not — which is why it is per graphic.

The Flight Recorder

playable
09

Scrub the show's causes, not its video

Production analyticsVisual debuggingReview tools
Everyone records the output. Nobody records the reason. Streamatrix journals every operation, take, data value, fault and frame cost with frame-accurate timing — so “why did the scoreboard say 2–1 at 67:14?” is a question you scrub to and answer, instead of a meeting.
SpecimenPremier League · Match Day 14 · drag the playhead
The record is the document. No extra capture, no separate log — the operation journal that already provides undo and crash recovery is the flight recorder. It costs almost nothing because it already exists.
Why does this exist?
Because after every incident, a room of people watch a recording of the output and argue about what caused it. The cause is knowable and nobody stores it.
Why has nobody done it?
Because in most architectures the state is spread across a renderer, a controller and a feed, with no common clock. Streamatrix has one document, one journal and one clock — the flight recorder is nearly free.
What problem does it solve?
Attribution. Which data value, from which feed, at which timestamp, taken by which operator, produced what the audience saw.
What trade-off?
It records who did what, which is a surveillance risk in a workplace. It must therefore be show-scoped, retention-limited, and visible to the operators it records — an operator can always see their own record. Any other design turns a safety tool into a management one, and it will be resented and disabled.
What doctrine?
Volume Two's blame-free incident report, made concrete. The report writes itself because the data is already there.
Ten years out
Post-show review changes from watching a tape to reading a timeline, and “we don't know why” stops being an acceptable answer.
Would a broadcaster miss it after six months?The first time something went wrong and they could not answer for it.
10

Visual Diff

version control for pixels
10

An update shows you the frames that changed, before you accept it

Package managementVersion controlReview tools
When a package updates, Streamatrix renders every affected graphic before and after, at the same timecodes, with the same data, and shows you a pixel diff of the graphics that will go to air. A changelog says “improved spacing”. A diff shows you the sponsor bar moved 14 px.
Why does this exist?
Because accepting a package update currently means trusting a stranger's release note about something that is about to be on television.
Why has nobody done it?
Because you need deterministic rendering to diff frames meaningfully, and most graphics systems are not deterministic enough to render the same frame twice. Streamatrix's projection is deterministic by architecture — this is a capability that falls out of ADR-013, not a feature bolted onto it.
What problem does it solve?
The silent visual regression. A template update that subtly breaks a graphic nobody checked, discovered on air three weeks later.
What trade-off?
Rendering both versions costs time and disk, and a pixel diff over-reports — antialiasing noise reads as change. It therefore diffs at a perceptual threshold and ranks by area affected, so the sponsor bar moving outranks a shifted anti-aliased edge.
What doctrine?
Volume Two's confidence chapter — “did it update?” is answered by showing the change, not by asserting it happened.
Ten years out
Accepting a graphics update without seeing its diff looks like merging a pull request without reading it.
Would a broadcaster miss it after six months?Yes — updating would start to feel dangerous again.

The Portable Desk

the machine becomes yours
11

Sit at any Streamatrix in the world and it becomes your instrument in under a second

Workspace behaviourOperator assistanceMulti-operator
Layout, bindings, density, finish, sound preferences and pinned panels travel with the operator's identity and reflow to whatever furniture they have sat down at — and the machine tells you it has become yours, because a silent transformation is indistinguishable from a machine that was already set up wrong.
Why does this exist?
Because operators move between desks constantly — a truck, a gallery, a hotel room — and every move currently costs the first twenty minutes of a shift.
Why has nobody done it?
Settings sync exists everywhere. What does not exist is sync that reflows: Volume Two's layouts are bound to role × form factor, so the same identity produces a correct-but-different workspace on a laptop and in a truck.
What problem does it solve?
Muscle memory is the operator's most valuable asset and it is currently destroyed by changing seat.
What trade-off?
It needs identity, and identity needs the network — which collides with “nothing needed during a show may require the internet”. Resolved by caching the desk locally on first sign-in: you can always work offline at a desk you have used before, and a new desk fails to your role's default rather than to nothing.
What doctrine?
Volume Two W8 — layouts belong to a role, never to a machine. This is the other half of that decision.
Ten years out
The desk is a terminal and the operator is the configuration, which is how galleries have always worked socially and never technically.
Would a broadcaster miss it after six months?Freelancers, who work at a different desk every week, would refuse to go back.

The Bounded Assistant

doctrine before features

Every product in this category will ship AI. Most will ship it badly, by letting it act. Streamatrix's position is that a model is a very good analyst and an unacceptable operator, and the entire design follows from taking that seriously rather than repeating it in marketing copy.

The bounded assistant law No model output may reach an audience without a human having seen the rendered result. No model may initiate an action, schedule one, or occupy a step in the live path. Assistance is proposal, verification and explanation — three things a model is genuinely good at — and never authority. This is not caution about capability. It is a statement about accountability: when a graphic is wrong on television, a person must be answerable for it, and that requires that a person chose it. A system where the honest answer is “the model did it” is a system no broadcaster can operate.
Where a model is allowedand what it may never touch
CapabilityWhat it doesBoundary
Contract inferenceReads a feed sample and proposes the Data Contract — types, ranges, required fields, glyph limits.Proposal only; the contract is confirmed by a person and thereafter enforced deterministically, not by the model.
Worst-case discoveryFinds the adversarial extremes in a dataset, including ones a human would not think of — diacritics, RTL names, ties, negative scores.It finds cases. The renderer decides whether they fit. No aesthetic judgement.
Understudy proposalSuggests degradations for a graphic based on what its bindings can fail to provide.Every understudy is reviewed rendered before it can be used. §06 plus §08.
Incident narrationTurns a Flight Recorder span into plain prose: what happened, in what order, and what the product tried.It may describe the record. It may not add causes that are not in the record, and every claim links to its journal entry.

Search and recall“The lower third we used for the Lagos correspondent in March” resolves to the graphic.Returns candidates, ranked, with why. Never opens or applies anything.

Attribution Anything a model proposed is marked as such for the life of the object, including in the Flight Recorder. A person accepted it — the record says who, and says the origin.
Why does this exist?
Because the useful applications here — inferring a contract, finding adversarial data, narrating an incident — are exactly the tedious work that stops broadcasters from doing the safety practices they already believe in.
Why has nobody done it?
Because the industry conversation is about generation, which is the one thing that must not touch the live path. Applying models to verification rather than creation is the unusual move.
What trade-off?
We will lose demos to competitors whose AI does more. We will also still be trusted after the first time one of those systems puts something wrong on air.
What doctrine?
I3, and Volume One's Law 6 — with Amendment 2, which this volume adds to make “a proposal is not a control” explicit.

Amendments

arguing with earlier volumes
Amendment 1 · Volume Two, §11 — Undo & air Was: “Undo never crosses the on-air boundary.” Stated as a limitation.
Now: The same rule, but reframed — the boundary is not a limit on undo, it is the point where undo becomes the Flight Recorder. The original was correct and incomplete, and the incompleteness made it sound like a shortcoming. You cannot undo a broadcast, but you can inspect it, attribute it and explain it — and that is the actual thing an operator wants when they say “what just happened?”. Undo and the record are one mechanism with a threshold in the middle, not a feature and a gap.
Amendment 2 · Volume One, Law 6 Was: “Never draw a control for something the engine cannot do.”
Now: Unchanged, with an added clause — a proposal is not a control, and must never be shaped like one. Volume Four introduces a class of object Law 6 did not anticipate: a suggestion. If a proposal is rendered as a button, Law 6 is violated in spirit — the interface is offering an action the product is not authorised to take. Proposals therefore have their own form: they are always shown as the result they would produce, accepted by an explicit act, and marked with their origin permanently. A proposal you can accept by muscle memory is a decision the machine made.
Amendment 3 · Volume Two, §12 — Marketplace voice Was: “It never uses star ratings out of five.”
Now: No subjective ratings. Objective operational telemetry is not only permitted but required. I was right to ban five stars and wrong to leave nothing in their place. A star rating measures popularity; a broadcaster needs fitness. A package should carry its measured frame cost (§03), its contract requirements (§04), whether it declares understudies (§06), its legal-check status (§05), and its update history with visual diffs (§10). That is a spec sheet, not a review — and it is the kind of thing Blackmagic puts on a box and nobody has ever put on a graphics template. Reported anonymously and in aggregate, never per-facility.

Risks

what could go wrong
R1
The safety features become theatre.A Dry Run that always passes, a legal check nobody reads, a verification step people click through. Every one of these decays into ritual unless it fails often enough to stay credible — and the discipline is to keep the checks that fail and delete the ones that never do.
R2
We build an analysis product and forget it is a graphics product.Nine of twelve inventions here are verification. That is the right emphasis for a mature product and the wrong one for a young one: a broadcaster will not adopt Streamatrix because it checks well. They adopt it because the graphics are good, and then stay because it checks well.
R3
The Flight Recorder becomes a management tool.The single largest cultural risk in this volume. If operators believe it is used to assign blame, it will be disabled or worked around, and the safety value evaporates. The mitigations are structural — show-scoped, retention-limited, operator-visible — but structure will not survive a determined manager.
R4
Contracts make authoring feel bureaucratic.Types are resented until they save you. If contract authoring is not almost entirely inferred, designers will avoid the feature and every dependent invention degrades with it.
R5
The refusals cost real revenue.“No automation to air” and “no generative content on the live path” will lose deals to competitors, and the loss will be visible and quarterly while the benefit is invisible and annual. This is the risk most likely to actually kill the doctrine.
R6
Twelve inventions is eleven too many for version one.Shipping these badly and simultaneously would produce a product that does many things approximately. The order matters more than the list: Data Contract, then Worst Case, then Dry Run — because the second two are worthless without the first.

Conformance

C31 – C40
C31
Nothing goes to air without a person.Search the codebase for every path that can reach the program bus. Each must originate in an input event.
C32
No model output reaches air unreviewed.Every AI-originated object carries an origin mark and a review state. Assert that unreviewed objects cannot be armed.
C33
Every invention can be disabled.Turn all twelve off per production. The product must remain coherent, and no surface may reference a disabled feature.
C34
The Dry Run states what it could not check.Run against an unavailable feed. The report must be explicit about the gap, not silently green.
C35
Worst Case uses real data.Point a graphic at a live feed and confirm the extreme is drawn from the feed, never from a synthetic string.
C36
Frame budget is pessimistic.The reported cost must be the heaviest frame in the rundown, and must never be an average.
C37
An understudy on air is always announced.Force every degradation. Each must produce an amber state on the object, visible without navigation.
C38
The Flight Recorder is visible to the recorded.Sign in as an operator. Their own record must be readable by them, in full, without a request.
C39
Verification requires rendered viewing.Attempt to approve a graphic without opening it. The approval must be impossible, not merely discouraged.
C40
The desk works offline.Sign in, disconnect, restart. The workspace must be identical.
16

Roadmap

the remaining volumes

Four volumes were named in the brief and are not written here. They are listed with what each must actually decide, because a titled volume with no thesis is worse than an unwritten one — it looks finished.

VOLUME FIVE

Workspace Intelligence

Must decide the hardest question in the product: how much may the workspace anticipate before it violates H2 — nothing moves unless a person moved it? Critical Path (§07) is the first crack in that wall and the volume has to resolve it properly.

VOLUME SIX

Marketplace & Asset Philosophy

Needs the package format: versioning, dependency resolution, integrity, provenance, and what a package promises. Amendment 3 sets the direction — a spec sheet, not a review — and the format has to carry it.

VOLUME SEVEN

Multi-Operator

Beyond Volume Two's write lock. Must answer whether two people can hold one show, how authority is transferred mid-broadcast, and what happens when the two of them disagree while something is on air.

VOLUME EIGHT

Accessibility

Deferred three times now, which is itself a decision I am not comfortable with. It needs the one thing this project has never had: real users. Everything else here can be reasoned from first principles; this cannot.

Volume Four ends here Twelve inventions, and the one that matters most is the cheapest: the Data Contract. Everything else in this volume either depends on it or is improved by it, and it is the least impressive thing to demonstrate. That is usually what a foundation looks like.
