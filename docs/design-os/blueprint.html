StudioFinal Blueprint

Less Studio

The previous specification was a good description of a conventional editor. Working through it against the real question — what does a designer actually do forty times a day? — the answer is that Studio has too much interface for the work it does.

This blueprint removes two of five permanent panels, demotes the timeline from the primary authoring surface to an escape hatch, and gives the viewport 76 % of the width. Building a lower third goes from 11 actions to 5. Nothing here is a feature; every change deletes work.

01

Critical Findings

against my own spec

Seven findings against the Studio Specification. Four of them are cases where I described what professional editors do rather than what this product needs.

B-01
The timeline was made the primary animation surface, and it should not be. A lower third's entire animation is rise and fade, glide, 12 frames, staggered by 3. That is five values. Expressing it as four lanes and eight keyframes is more work than saying it. I specified a beautiful timeline and then routed the easy case — which is 90 % of broadcast graphics — through the hard path. Resolution: §03, motion becomes a property.
B-02
Two permanent panels describe the selected object, on opposite sides of the screen. Layers on the left, Inspector on the right, canvas between. Over eight hours the eye crosses the viewport constantly to relate a thing to its description. Resolution: §02, one right dock — navigator above, properties below. The left dock is deleted and the viewport gains 208 px.
B-03
A show delivers three formats and the spec only ever showed one. 1080p50 for programme, 2160p50 for the archive, 9:16 for social. The designer checks each by switching, which means most graphics are never checked in most formats. Resolution: §05, a permanent confidence strip rendering every declared format live.
B-04
Components were never specified, so a facility with forty lower thirds has forty graphics. They differ by text and colour. That is one component and forty data rows. Without components the Marketplace, the brand kit and the update model in Volume Six all operate on the wrong unit. Resolution: §04.
B-05
Touchpad behaviour was never specified, and most authoring happens on a laptop. Pinch, two-finger pan and momentum have different physics from a mouse wheel, and a spec that only describes a wheel will produce a tool that fights half its users. Resolution: §06.
B-06
Live data changes the screen without a person, which H2 forbids. A bound text layer's content changes when the feed changes. That is the interface moving on its own, and no document addressed it. Resolution: §11, Amendment 1 — permitted, and marked.
B-07
The History panel exists because editors have one. Undo is ⌘Z; what happened during a show is the Flight Recorder; what happened to the document is version marks. A scrolling list of every property change serves none of those and occupies a tab. Resolution: §09, removed.

03

Motion as Property

playable · the central change

Broadcast graphics are built from a small vocabulary of entrances. Not because designers lack imagination, but because an entrance has to be readable in twelve frames on a moving background, and the set of things that work is genuinely small. Every facility rebuilds that vocabulary by hand, in keyframes, thousands of times.

M-1 · animation is a property with a name A layer's motion is authored as preset · ease · frames, and a group's stagger is a single number. The keyframes are generated, visible, and editable — but nobody places them to get the common case. This is not a shortcut layered over a timeline. It is the primary representation: the document stores the preset, and the timeline is a view of what the preset produced. Breaking out to hand-keyframes is one action and is recorded on the layer, so a reviewer can see which layers left the vocabulary and ask why.
M-0 · the golden rule · Volume One L8 Motion-as-property is not a feature. It is the first instance of the rule the whole product now follows: every professional capability ships with a one-click version that is the default, and the advanced controls are the option. The pattern generalises exactly. Enable depth provisions the camera, the lighting, the environment, the material and the shadows. Glass builds transmission, IOR, reflections, roughness and fresnel. Broadcast ready runs the pre-flight, the overflow check, the contracts, output validation and the frame budget. Responsive generates constraints, scaling and anchors. Social outputs creates 16:9, 9:16, 1:1 and 4:5 with confidence previews. One press; hundreds of decisions. In every case the generated result is inspectable and editable, and breaking out is one action that is recorded on the object.

demoted, not deleted

Two things the previous timeline got wrong

T-5
HOLD needed a vocabulary, not just a rule. I said HOLD may contain loops and data reactions and then specified neither. A scoreboard in HOLD does three things: a loop (a shimmer, a ticker), a data reaction (the score changes and the number flips), and a state swap (possession moves side). Those are the three, they are declarative like the entrance presets, and they are the only things permitted in HOLD.
T-6
Procedural motion was ignored entirely. A ticker scrolls at a rate, not through keyframes. A count-up interpolates to a bound value whose target is unknown at design time. Neither is expressible as a keyframe and both are common. They are properties of the layer with a rate and a bound target, shown in the timeline as a hatched band rather than a segment — visibly not keyframed, because it is not.
05

Components

the missing unit

A facility does not have forty lower thirds. It has one lower third and forty rows of data — and until the product models that, every downstream system operates on the wrong object: brand kits apply forty times, package updates diff forty graphics, and a designer fixes a spacing error forty times.

C-1
A component is a graphic that declares a surface. Its surface is the set of properties an instance may override — usually the bound variables, sometimes a colour role or a mark. Everything else is structural and cannot be overridden by an instance. The surface is declared, not inferred, so a component author knows exactly what they are promising.
C-2
An instance shows its overrides, always, and can reset each one. An overridden property carries a mark in the inspector and a one-action reset. Why: the failure mode of every component system is the instance that silently diverged eight months ago and now ignores the brand update.
C-3
Editing an instance never edits the component. There is no ambiguity, no "edit main", no push-to-master. Changing the component is a deliberate act performed in the component, and it shows how many instances will change and diffs them — Volume Six's machinery, applied inside the facility.
C-4
A component with no overrides in use is not a component. Studio reports components whose instances all match. That usually means the variation should have been data, and it is worth telling someone.
C-5 · depth is a property Any 2D object may be given depth. A rectangle becomes an extruded rectangle, text becomes extruded text, a logo becomes an extruded logo. Nothing becomes a mesh. Nothing leaves the document model. Everything stays editable as the 2D object it still is — the Stage remains screen-space and a designer still drags rectangles exactly as they did before. 3D is an engine capability, not a workflow, and there is no "3D mode" to enter or leave. Per M-0 the engine provisions the camera, lighting, environment and material on the same press.
06

Confidence Strip

every format, always

Broadcast has always had confidence monitors — a wall of small screens showing every feed, so a fault is seen rather than reported. The design tool has never had one, and the result is graphics checked in one format and transmitted in three.

V-2 · every declared format renders continuously The stage shows the primary format large and every other declared delivery format as a live render beside it — same data, same frame, same engine. It costs real GPU time, which is why it is bounded: a maximum of four secondary formats, rendered at 1/6 scale, at 10 fps while authoring and paused entirely while an output is live. A format whose render fails a check — overflow, illegal colour, an element outside title safe — carries an attention mark, and clicking it makes that format primary.

07

Variants, not Infinite Canvas

the request, answered differently

Infinite canvas was asked for directly, and the Studio spec refused it — there is no space outside the frame, because a pasteboard is where forgotten objects go to be rendered accidentally at 20:00. I still think that refusal is right, but it was an answer to the mechanism rather than to the need.

What people actually want a pasteboard for is comparison. Two versions of a lower third side by side. A dark and a light treatment. The graphic with the longest name and with the shortest.

V-3 · variants are real, framed and switchable A graphic may hold named variants. Each is a complete frame at delivery resolution, not a region of a larger canvas. They are shown side by side on request, and exactly one is the graphic that airs. This gives comparison without giving a place to park rubbish. A variant is either promoted or deleted; it cannot linger in a corner. And because every variant is a real frame, all of it — worst case, legal check, frame cost — applies to each one, which a pasteboard region could never support.

High DPI & Monitors

the 1:1 promise
D-1
100 % means one output pixel per physical device pixel. On a 2× display the canvas is drawn at 2× into a backing store and presented at half CSS size, so a 1920-wide frame occupies 960 points and every output pixel is a real pixel. Why: the alternative — 1920 CSS points — makes 100 % a lie on exactly the displays designers use, and type legibility judged there is wrong.
D-2
A fractional scale factor never produces a fractional canvas. At 150 %, the canvas snaps to the nearest integer device-pixel size and the surrounding layout absorbs the remainder. A half-pixel canvas edge is a shimmering hairline on every redraw.
D-3
Moving the window between displays re-renders at the new density before it is shown. Never a scaled bitmap for a frame or two. The blur is brief and it teaches designers to distrust what they see.
D-4
The second monitor's job in Studio is a full-frame confidence render. Not a copy of Studio — the graphic, alone, at 100 %, with no chrome and no cursor after 2 s, over the live feed if one is configured. This is Volume Two G3 applied to authoring, and it is the single most valuable use of a second screen in this workflow.

Design OS Amendments

three
Amendment 1 · Volume Two H2, via Volume Five Was: Nothing the operator can see changes unless a person changed it.
Now: …except a value bound to live data, which changes when the data changes and is marked when it does. B-06. A bound score updating is the product working, not the interface moving. But an unmarked change is indistinguishable from a mistake, so the rule gains its exception and its obligation together: the value changes, and a one-frame accent tick on the field says it was the feed. No motion, no layout change, no reflow — the number is replaced and the field acknowledges it.
Amendment 2 · Volume Two W6 Was: Auto-hide does not exist. No panel slides away and returns.
Now: Unchanged for docked panels. A summoned transient is not auto-hide. The timeline opening on ⌥T and closing on Escape is deliberate in both directions and never a function of pointer position, which is what W6 was written against. The distinction that matters: a panel that appears because you asked is a tool; a panel that appears because your mouse arrived is a hazard.
Amendment 3 · Studio Specification §04 T-1 Was: HOLD may contain loops and data reactions.
Now: HOLD contains exactly three declarable things — a loop, a data reaction, or a state swap — and nothing else. T-5. The original was a permission with no vocabulary, which in practice would have become "anything, as long as you call it a loop". Naming the three closes it, and all three are declarative like the entrance presets rather than keyframed, because none of them has a known duration.

Engineering Notes

what this costs to build
E-1
Motion presets are document data, not a UI convenience. The scene format stores motion: { preset, ease, frames, stagger } on the layer, and the projection expands it to keyframes at load. This is additive under SCENE_FORMAT §13 rule 4 — an optional property with a default — so it needs no version bump. Hand-keyframed layers store keyframes as today; the two coexist and a layer is one or the other.
E-2
The confidence strip is N extra projections, not N extra documents. One document, one mirror per format, each with its own viewport dimensions. The engine's determinism makes them consistent by construction. Budget: 4 secondaries × 1/6 scale × 10 fps ≈ 11 % of one full-rate render. Suspended entirely when an output is live, per Volume Five P2.
E-3
Components need one new document concept and no new engine concept. An instance is a reference plus an override map. The projection resolves it before reconciliation, so the mirror never knows components exist. This keeps ADR-013 untouched, which is the point.
E-4
The summoned timeline must not cost a layout pass. It composites over the stage rather than resizing it. Resizing the stage would re-project every format in the confidence strip, which is exactly the hitch a summoned panel must not have.
E-5
Device detection needs no permissions and no settings. wheelDeltaY magnitude plus event phase distinguishes trackpad from wheel reliably enough, and the failure mode of getting it wrong is a slightly odd zoom rather than a broken tool. Do not add a preference; a preference means every user must discover they need it.
E-6
The data tick must originate in the projection, not the UI. The field flashes because the projection told it a bound value changed, not because the UI diffed a string. A UI-side diff would fire on re-render and lie.

Honest Limitations

and open questions
L-1
Motion presets will not cover the flagship graphic. A title sequence or a complex scoreboard reveal needs hand keyframes, and those designers will experience the preset vocabulary as a wall they have to climb over on every layer. The break-out is one action, but it is one action they perform constantly. Unresolved: whether a facility should be able to make hand-keyframing the default for a graphic.
L-2
Removing the left dock will be unpopular for about a week. Every professional editor has a left panel and hands expect one. I believe the ergonomic argument is correct and I am aware that believing it is not evidence.
L-3
The confidence strip's cost estimate is arithmetic, not measurement. 11 % assumes linear scaling with pixel count, which is wrong for anything fill-bound. On a text-heavy graphic it will be cheaper; on a blur it could be far worse.
L-4
Text editing is still not specified. Named as the one real hole in the Studio Specification and it remains one. Selection semantics, bidirectional text, ligature-aware caret movement. Nothing in this blueprint touches it, and it is the most-used tool in the product.
L-5
Variants multiply everything. Four variants × four formats is sixteen renders of one graphic, sixteen worst-case checks and sixteen legal checks. The budget in E-2 does not account for variants, and the honest answer is that variants and the confidence strip probably cannot both be at full fidelity simultaneously.
L-6
Still nothing has been built. Unchanged and unchangeable from a desk. Every action count in this document is derived, not observed.
Workflow, re-countedthe proof that the removals paid
Estimates, stated as acceptance criteria. If an implementation cannot hit these, the interface is wrong regardless of how it looks.
17

The Question

one only
If Apple acquired Streamatrix tomorrow, what would they still redesign before shipping Studio?

Text. Not the timeline, not the panels, not the viewport — those are decided and defensible. They would open a graphic, double-click a name, and find that the single most-used interaction in the entire product is the one thing this project never specified. Then they would find that it needs a real text engine underneath — shaping, bidi, ligature-aware carets, grapheme-cluster selection — and that the MSDF pipeline was built to render text, not to let a person work inside it. That is not a design gap, it is a missing subsystem, and it is the thing that would delay a ship date.

Second, they would take the vocabulary away from the operator. Every document in this project is proud of its precision — tethers, bands, contracts, impact classes, the shaped lamp — and a designer opening Studio for the first time meets all of it at once. Apple would keep every mechanism and hide most of the words, because a system that requires you to learn its terminology before you can make a lower third has put its own coherence ahead of the person using it.

Third, and most uncomfortably: they would ask why Studio and the control room are the same application. This whole project has assumed one product spanning authoring and transmission, and has spent enormous effort — the show clock, the token, the sterile phase, the entire on-air/off-air bifurcation of the keyboard — reconciling two jobs with genuinely opposing requirements. Apple would probably split them, ship the authoring tool first, and let the control room be a smaller, harder, separate thing. I think that would be the wrong call for a broadcaster and the right call for a business, and I do not know which of those matters more.
