Volume FiveWorkspace Intelligence

Knowing without deciding

Volume Two set a rule that has been quietly straining ever since: nothing moves unless a person moved it. Volume Four then introduced a workspace that predicts what you will need and prepares it. Those two things are in tension, and pretending otherwise would leave the most consequential behaviour in the product to be decided ad hoc by whoever implements it first.

This volume resolves it, and then does something no broadcast tool does: it makes the show clock a first-class input to the product's behaviour. Streamatrix knows how close it is to air, and it gets quieter as the number gets smaller.

01

The Tension

and its resolution

Volume Two, rule H2: “Nothing moves unless a person moved it. No auto-scroll, no auto-select, no reordering under the pointer, no list that re-sorts when data arrives.” The reasoning was fatigue — a tired operator clicking where a thing was two seconds ago must hit the thing.

Volume Four, invention 07: Critical Path — the product measures each graphic's readiness latency, predicts where the operator is going, and pre-warms. I noted at the time that this was “the first crack in that wall”. It is worth being precise about why it is not actually a crack.

The resolving law · W-I The workspace may prepare anything and rearrange nothing. Preparation changes what the machine has ready. Presentation changes what the operator sees. H2 governs presentation absolutely and says nothing about preparation — and the whole of workspace intelligence lives on the preparation side of that line. A decoded image sitting in GPU memory has moved nothing. A panel that slid because the product thought you would want it has moved everything.

This gives a test any proposed intelligence must pass, and it is blunt enough to settle an argument in a review: if the feature were switched off mid-show, would the screen look different? If yes, it is presentation and H2 forbids it. If no — the screen is identical and only the machine's readiness changed — it is preparation and it is permitted.

The corollary that keeps it honest Everything prepared is listed. There is no invisible readiness. Speculation the operator cannot inspect is a machine with a private opinion about the show. The preparation ledger (§03) is always open to look at, shows exactly what has been warmed and why, and can be emptied by hand. A prediction you can audit is a tool; a prediction you cannot is a haunting.
02

The Show Clock

drag it

Every broadcast has a time it starts. Every production tool knows this and almost none of them do anything with it. Streamatrix treats proximity to air as a governing input: the same product, with the same layout, behaves differently at T−4h and T−4m — not by moving anything, but by changing what it runs in the background, what it will permit, and how much it is willing to say.

Six phases. Drag the playhead below and watch the product change its own posture. Note how narrow Lock is — ten minutes at the end of a five-hour run-up. The strip is drawn to scale on purpose.

The layout never changes. Not once, across all six phases. Only posture changes — what runs, what is permitted, how loud.
The opt-in rule The clock constrains nothing until a person declares an air time. A production with no scheduled air stays in Prep for ever and behaves exactly as Volume Two describes. This matters more than it sounds: it means workspace intelligence is switched on by an act every broadcaster already performs, and never by a setting nobody finds. It also means a designer at 14:00 on a Tuesday with no show booked is never told what they may not do.
Lock is a fact, not a mode Volume Two's H1 forbids invisible modes. Lock is permitted because the clock is the mode indicator — the largest, most prominent readout in the product, showing exactly why the machine is behaving as it is. An operator may always override a Lock refusal. The override is one action, it is never buried, and it is recorded in the Flight Recorder with the operator's identity — not as a reprimand, but because the next person to ask “why did the package update six minutes before air?” deserves an answer.
03

Preparation

the ledger

What the machine is allowed to do on its own initiative, and the accounting that keeps it honest.

Preparation ledgeralways open · always emptiable by hand 1.8 GB of 4.0 GB budget
Why a ledger and not a setting — an operator cannot trust a prediction engine they cannot inspect. This is the inspection.
P1
Preparation has a declared budget and never exceeds it. A fixed share of GPU and system memory, set per machine, visible in the ledger. When full, preparation refuses rather than evicting something the show may need — the same rule the asset library already follows for exactly the same reason.
P2
Preparation never competes with the live path. All speculative work runs at lower priority than anything feeding an output, and is suspended entirely while a frame is late. Why: an optimisation that can cause the failure it exists to prevent is not an optimisation.
P3
Eviction order is declared, not learned. Speculative first, then cold caches, then previews, then thumbnails. Never the live scene, never an on-air asset, never anything in the next three rundown items. A learned eviction policy is unpredictable under pressure, which is precisely when it matters.
P4
A wrong prediction costs nothing but memory. Preparing item 15 when the operator jumps to item 3 wastes some bandwidth and no correctness. This is the property that makes speculation safe here and unsafe almost everywhere else: the speculative work has no side effects. If a prediction could change the document, it would be forbidden.
P5
Readiness is a readout, not a promise. The rundown shows what is ready, what is warming and what is cold. It never hides a cold item behind an optimistic state, and taking a cold item is always permitted — it is simply slower, and the product says by how much beforehand.
04

Attention

a budget, not a feed

The second scarce resource in a gallery, and the one software is worst at respecting. An operator can hold roughly four things in working memory; under stress, fewer. Most software treats notifications as a queue to be drained — if six things happen, six things are shown. That is a design that works beautifully in a demo with two events and fails completely on the night when it matters.

The attention budget · W-II At most three demands may be presented at once. A fourth does not stack — it collapses the set into a single aggregate that states the count and the worst severity. This inverts the usual failure. Conventional software degrades from “manageable” to “overwhelming” exactly as the situation gets worse. Streamatrix degrades from “three specific problems” to “one problem that says there are six” — which is less information and far more usable, because the operator's next action is the same either way: open the list.

Order is severity, then age. Never recency alone — the newest event is rarely the most important one. On air the budget drops to one, and only show-affecting demands qualify.

Routing attention without moving anything

If the workspace may not rearrange itself, how does it point? The answer is the answer Volume One already gave for state: light, not motion. Four mechanisms, in escalating order, and none of them relocates a single pixel.

The four routesescalating, and each is reversible by resolution alone
RouteMechanismUsed when
1 · ObjectA lamp on the thing itself — the output row, the rundown item, the layer.Always. Every demand has an object, and the object always carries it.
2 · ContainerThe panel's collapsed spine inherits the worst lamp inside it.When the object is not currently visible. This is why panels collapse to a spine rather than hiding.

Never steal focus, scroll to the problem, open the panel, flash the taskbar, or play a sound that repeats. All four are motion, and motion is forbidden by H2.
05

The Learned Desk

observation without adaptation

The product watches how you work. It never acts on what it sees. That sentence is the whole design, and the temptation to soften it will be constant.

W-III Learned behaviour is offered in one place, at a moment of the operator's choosing, and is never applied. Observations surface only inside Desk — a settings surface an operator opens deliberately. They never appear as a popup, a banner, a nudge, a tip, an inline suggestion, or a highlighted control. If the operator never opens Desk, the product's learning is invisible for ever, and that is an acceptable outcome. A tool that adapts under you is a tool you have to re-learn every week.
What it may observe, and what it may offer
ObservationOfferNever

06

Idle Work

while nobody is touching it

A production machine is idle for most of its life, and that idle time is the cheapest resource in the building. Streamatrix spends it on the checks that would otherwise have to happen when someone is waiting — which is the whole of Volume Four's earliness doctrine, applied to the machine's own spare capacity.

I1
Idle work stops the instant a person touches anything. Not throttles — stops, at the next safe point, within one frame. The operator must never contend with the machine's own housekeeping, and a product that stutters when you touch it after an hour of quiet has taught you that it is busy with something more important than you.
I2
Idle work never changes the document. It verifies, measures, renders and reports. It does not fix, tidy, migrate, re-encode or optimise. Anything that would alter what goes to air waits for a person, however obviously correct it is.
I3
Its results are silent until asked for, unless something failed. A clean idle pass produces no notification, no badge and no toast — it simply means the standing verdict stays green. Reporting success that nobody requested is how a product teaches you to ignore it.
I4
It respects the machine, not just the operator. Suspended on battery, on thermal throttle, and whenever another Streamatrix on the same machine is on air. A laptop that runs hot in a hotel room the night before a show is a real failure with a real cost.
What idle time buysin the order it is spent

All of it is re-run on any change, incrementally. The standing verdict is never stale, and never claims to be fresher than it is — it carries the time it was computed.
07

What We Refuse

the popular version of this feature

Never

  • An adaptive interface. No panel that grows because you use it, no control that surfaces because you are near it, no menu ordered by frequency. Adaptation destroys muscle memory, which is the operator's most valuable asset.
  • Focus stealing. Nothing takes the keyboard. Ever. Not for an error, not for a modal, not for a critical fault.
  • Auto-scroll to the problem. The rundown does not jump. The lamp tells you where; you go there.
  • Predictive take. The product will never pre-select, pre-arm, or pre-cue. Warming is invisible; arming is a decision.
  • Tips, tours, nudges, coach marks, “did you know”. A professional tool teaches through consistency and printed bindings, not through interruption.
  • Cloud-side learning. An individual's working patterns never leave the facility.
  • A confidence score shown to the operator. “87 % likely you will take item 12” invites the operator to reason about the model instead of the show.

Instead

  • A layout that is identical on every machine, every day, for every operator in that role.
  • A machine that is quietly ready for what you are about to do, and says so as a readout you can check.
  • Escalating light that points without moving.
  • A clock that makes the product calmer as the stakes rise, rather than busier.
  • Learning that waits to be asked.

Known failure modesnamed, with the specific defence
FailureHow it happensDefence
Speculation stormThe predictor warms everything because it is uncertain, and starves the live path.P1 and P2 — hard budget, lower priority, suspended while a frame is late.

09

Risks

honest
R1
The show clock is the most invasive idea in the whole Design OS. It gives the product an opinion about what you may do and when. If the phase boundaries are wrong — and I have chosen them from reasoning, not evidence — it will feel like an obstruction rather than a colleague. This is the single decision here most likely to need changing after real shows.
R2
Lock will be experienced as the product blaming the operator. “You cannot install this, it is six minutes to air” is correct and slightly humiliating. The wording carries the entire outcome, and wording is the thing most likely to be got wrong by whoever implements it.
R3
The attention budget can hide a real emergency. Three shown, three collapsed — and the fourth was the one that mattered. The severity ordering mitigates it, but only if severity is classified correctly, which pushes the risk into Volume Two's failure classes rather than removing it.
R4
“Prepares but never rearranges” is easy to state and hard to police. A readiness lamp is a presentation change. I have permitted it because it reports rather than relocates, but that is a judgement, and the line will be pushed at every review by someone with a reasonable case.
R5
Refusing adaptation is a competitive liability in a demo. “It learns how you work” sells. “It refuses to change under you” has to be explained. This volume will lose an evaluation to a product that adapts visibly, and be preferred by the same evaluator six months later.
10

Amendments

correcting earlier volumes
Amendment 1 · Volume Two, H2 Was: “Nothing moves unless a person moved it.”
Now: “Nothing the operator can see changes unless a person changed it. What the machine has made ready may change freely, and is always listed.” The original was written about scrolling and re-sorting and is right about those. It was also, read literally, a ban on caching — which nobody intended and which would have made the product slower for no benefit. The amendment draws the line where it always should have been: at the boundary between presentation and preparation, not at the boundary between still and moving.
Amendment 2 · Volume Four, invention 07 Was: Critical Path “predicts where the operator is going”.
Now: Critical Path measures readiness and warms along the declared rundown order. It does not model the operator. I was careless. “Predicting the operator” invites a behavioural model of a person, which is both unnecessary and the beginning of every adaptive-interface failure in §07. The rundown is an explicit, human-authored declaration of what comes next — warming along it needs no prediction at all, and is correct by construction rather than correct on average. The only genuine prediction left is how far ahead to warm, which is a function of pace and memory, not of personality.

12

Open Questions

needs shows, not thinking
Q1
Are the six phases the right six?Four hours, one hour, ten minutes. I chose these from how production days are described, not from measurement. A news operation and a stadium sports operation may need different boundaries, which would make the phase table a per-facility policy rather than a product constant.
Q2
Is three the right attention budget?The working-memory research supports about four items; three is a deliberate margin. Whether an experienced director under pressure can hold more, or fewer, is measurable and unmeasured.
Q3
What happens when the show runs late?The whole model assumes air time is known and roughly accurate. Live sport routinely runs 40 minutes over. Whether the clock should follow the schedule or the actual state of the output is genuinely unresolved, and the two disagree exactly when it matters.
Q4
Should a rehearsal have its own clock?Rehearsals are shows without an audience. They probably deserve Lock without the sterile phase — but that is a fourth kind of “live”, and Volume Two's state model already distinguishes three.
Q5
Does the ledger get looked at?I have argued that an inspectable prediction is trustworthy and an opaque one is not. It is equally possible that nobody opens it once, and that the honest conclusion would then be to remove the speculation rather than keep an unread audit trail.
Volume Five ends here The hardest question in this volume was not what the workspace should know. It was what it should be allowed to do with what it knows — and the answer, consistently, is less than it could.
