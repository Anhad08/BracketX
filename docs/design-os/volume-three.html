Volume ThreeSound

The Machine Language

Streamatrix has no chimes, no notification tones and no brand sound. It has a machine — an imagined object of milled aluminium, sealed relays, a magnetic detent and one small flywheel — and every sound the product makes is a thing that object could physically do.

This constraint is not romantic. It is what makes twenty-six sounds cohere into one instrument instead of twenty-six decisions, and it is what lets an operator identify an event through programme audio, in headphones, without looking. That last capability is the entire point of this volume.

Sound is off until you turn it on
Every sound below is synthesised from its specification when you press this — there are no audio files in this document and nothing is fetched. Use headphones if you have them: the stereo field carries meaning, and it is the part of this system that is hardest to appreciate on laptop speakers. Several voices are deliberately very quiet.
01

Doctrine

eight laws of sound

Volume One's laws govern; these extend them into the audible. Every one of the eight is falsifiable — you can play a build and say it was broken.

S1
Sound marks transitions. Only light holds state. Nothing in Streamatrix loops, pulses, repeats or sustains — ever, including alarms. Why: a repeating alert becomes the room's background within about ninety seconds and stops being information, which is the mechanism behind every "alarm fatigue" death in medicine. State is held by a lamp, which never fatigues because it never demands.
S2
Streamatrix speaks below the voice and above it, never through it. Critical events place their energy under 200 Hz and over 3.5 kHz, avoiding the 300 Hz–3.4 kHz band where speech intelligibility lives. Why: an operator's ears already contain programme audio and talkback. A sound that competes with the director's voice will either be missed or will mask the instruction — both are failures, and the second is dangerous.
S3
Every sound implies a mechanism. If you cannot say which part of the imagined machine made it, it is not a Streamatrix sound. Why: physical plausibility is what makes a set of sounds feel like one object. It also constrains invention usefully: the machine has no bell, so there is no way to add a cheerful ping later.
S4
Position encodes source. Preview events sit left, program events right, system events centre. Why: a director in headphones can tell which bus an event belongs to before they have identified the sound. This is the highest-bandwidth channel available and almost nobody uses it.
S5
Consequence is carried by weight, not by volume. The take is not louder than a detent; it is lower. Sub-100 Hz content is reserved for exactly two events. Why: loudness is fought over and turned down. Weight survives a volume knob, and it can be felt through a desk.
S6
No sound is required to complete a task. Sound is a second channel, never the only one, and the product is fully operable with the speakers unplugged. Why: deaf and hard-of-hearing operators work in this industry, most galleries run silent, and any design that leans on audio will fail in both cases.
S7
The machine never speaks first. Sound follows an operator action or a genuine change of world state. Nothing sounds because a timer elapsed, because a suggestion is available, or because the product would like attention. Why: the moment software makes an unprompted noise it becomes a colleague who interrupts, and operators mute colleagues who interrupt.
S8
On air, the machine goes quiet — with two exceptions. Everything ducks by 12 dB or mutes entirely while on air, except Critical fault and Take. Why: aviation's sterile-cockpit rule. Take stays because the operator must hear their own commit; Critical stays because the alternative is silence during the one event that requires a person.
02

The Machine

six mechanisms

Before any sound was designed, the object that makes them was. It is a piece of equipment that does not exist, described precisely enough that a sound designer joining in 2036 can extend the language without inventing a new one.

The instrument is a milled 4 mm aluminium chassis, hard-mounted, filled rather than hollow. It contains sealed relays, a magnetic detent shuttle running on a short rail, a small mass-loaded flywheel, and one pressure valve. It has no bells, no springs that ring, no resonant cavities and no speaker of its own. Everything in it is damped. Nothing it does rings for longer than 340 ms, and only one thing it does goes below 100 Hz.

The six mechanismsclick any one to hear it in isolation
Heard alone these are not sounds, they are raw material. Every voice in §04 is one or more of them, positioned and weighted. Not in the machine: bells, chimes, marimbas, pads, risers, whooshes, reverb tails, musical intervals, anything with a key signature.
The extension test A new sound may join the language only if you can name which of the six mechanisms produced it. "It should feel friendlier" is not a mechanism. If a needed event has no plausible mechanism, the machine gains a mechanism by amendment — deliberately, once, documented — rather than the language quietly acquiring a seventh voice nobody agreed to.
03

Spectrum & Field

where the machine speaks

The single most consequential decision in this volume, and the one that would be hardest for a competitor to copy without rebuilding their whole sound set: Streamatrix deliberately avoids the frequency band that human speech occupies.

An operator's ears are not empty. They contain programme audio, talkback, a director counting, and sometimes a floor manager. All of that lives between roughly 300 Hz and 3.4 kHz. A conventional UI sound — a mid-range chime around 800 Hz to 2 kHz — sits directly on top of it, which means the sound is either missed, or heard instead of the director. Both are failures. The second one gets someone's cue missed on television.

What We Refuse

and why

Never in Streamatrix

  • A brand sound. A startup signature is an advert played to a professional every morning. The product earns recognition by behaving well, not by announcing itself.
  • Musical intervals. A rising major third is emotional colouring, and it dates. The machine has no key signature, so it cannot go out of fashion.
  • Repeating alarms. S1. The single most dangerous convention in operational software.
  • Reverb. It implies a room. The instrument is on the desk, not across the hall.
  • Voice. No spoken alerts. Speech masks the director, which is the one thing that must never happen.
  • Sound as reward. No flourish for finishing, no fanfare for a purchase. Delight-per-use becomes irritation-per-thousand-uses.
  • Randomised variation. Some products vary a click slightly to avoid "machine-gunning". We do not: an operator must be able to tell two identical events apart from two different ones, and variation destroys that.

Instead

  • Silence as the default state. A well-running show is nearly silent. Sound is the exception, which is what gives it meaning.
  • Rate limiting, not variation. Identical events within 40 ms collapse into one. The tenth item selected in a fast scroll does not fire ten detents.
  • Mechanism, then emotion. The emotion is a consequence of the physics being right, never applied on top of it.
  • A quiet machine that gets quieter under pressure. The busier the show, the less Streamatrix says.

Failure Modes

how a sound system breaks

Sound systems in professional software fail in predictable ways. Naming them is how we avoid arriving at each one by accident over six releases.

Accessibility

the parallel channel

Volume Two deferred accessibility twice. This volume settles the audio half of it, because a sound system is the one place where an accessibility failure is total rather than partial — if meaning is carried only by sound, a deaf operator does not get a degraded experience, they get nothing.

The parallel channel rule Every sound has a named visual counterpart that fires on the same frame and is sufficient alone. The audio channel may be removed entirely with no loss of capability, no loss of confidence, and no change to timing. Sound is redundancy, and redundancy is exactly what makes it valuable to the operators who can hear it.

Amendments

arguing with Volumes One and Two

Three previous decisions do not survive contact with this volume. Each is amended here rather than quietly worked around, because a design system that cannot be corrected in public becomes a design system that is corrected in private.

Amendment 1 · Volume One, Law 2 Was: “Attack is fast, release is slow.”
Now: “Attack is fast. Release is slow, unless the release itself carries information.”
The original is wrong for faults. When a network fault clears, a slow visual decay says “this is still somewhat true” for nearly a second — which is a lie about the state of the world. Resolution must be as fast as onset, because an alarm that lingers after it is resolved teaches operators to distrust alarms. The slow release stays where it belongs: endings, not corrections.
Amendment 2 · Volume One, §04 Was: “Success is silent.”
Now: “Success is wordless. It is never silent in evidence, and it may be audible.”
The original conflated two things and the conflation was hiding a real hole. “Success is silent” was meant to ban the “Saved!” toast — correct. But taken literally it left the operator who is looking at a monitor wall with no confirmation at all, which is precisely the doubt the receipt doctrine exists to remove. Success produces no words and no dismissible message; it produces a visible state change, and where the operator's eyes are legitimately elsewhere, a sound.
Amendment 3 · Volume Two, Rule K4 Was: “No chords, no leader keys, no sequences.” — applied to the whole product.
Now: Applies to live operation only. Authoring may use sequences, provided the pending state is visible and cancellable.
I over-extended a good rule. A halfway state is dangerous under pressure — true, and that is why it stays banned for live. But a designer laying out graphics at 14:00 is not under pressure, and denying them a leader key costs a large, genuinely useful command space to protect against a risk that is not present. The condition is that a pending sequence is shown — a visible pending indicator with the keys so far, cancelled by Escape — and that no live command can ever be behind one. Sound reinforces it: an uncompleted sequence resolves with the Undo valve, so an abandoned chord is audibly nothing rather than ambiguously something.

Conformance

C23 – C30
C23
Nothing loops.Trigger every fault class and leave it unresolved for ten minutes. No sound may repeat.
C24
Critical energy avoids the voice band.Spectrum-analyse every voice marked critical. Energy between 300 Hz and 3.4 kHz must be at least 12 dB below the voice's peak band.
C25
The event-to-voice map is injective.No two operationally significant events share a voice. Assert it in a test, not by listening.
C26
Every voice has a named visual counterpart.Mute all audio and perform every task. No capability, and no confidence, may be lost.
C27
Latency is under 20 ms.Measure input-to-first-sample under load, on the slowest supported machine, with a show running.
C28
On air, only Take and Critical sound.Go live and exercise the entire product. Anything else that makes a noise is a defect.
C29
Batches sound once.Import 500 assets. Exactly one Asset imported voice may fire, at completion.
C30
Interface audio never reaches the programme bus.Record the programme mix while operating. The machine must be inaudible in it.

Open Questions

honest limits

What this volume does not know. Each is a real uncertainty rather than unfinished writing, and each needs evidence from a gallery rather than another afternoon of design.

Q1
Does S4 survive a real headset?Stereo position is elegant in a quiet room. Whether it is perceptible through a single-ear talkback headset with programme audio in it is an empirical question I cannot answer from a desk. If it fails, the fallback is duration coding, and it is specified but untested.
Q2
Is Take the right sound to keep on air?S8 keeps it because an operator must hear their own commit. But a director taking twenty graphics in a fast sequence hears twenty relays, and that may cross from confirmation into noise. The alternative — take is silent on air, confirmed only by tally — is defensible and I am genuinely unsure.
Q3
Should the flywheel be audible while running?Currently processes sound at start and end only, per S1. But a render that runs for four minutes with no audible presence is indistinguishable from one that died. The visual channel covers it; whether that is enough when your eyes are elsewhere is unresolved.
Q4
Facility-wide sound identity.Should a broadcaster be able to tune the machine — a slightly different chassis resonance per facility, the way studios have room tone? It would be beautiful, and it would let two Streamatrix desks in the same gallery be told apart. It also risks the language fragmenting. Not decided.
Q5
The 26 are not proven complete.They cover what the product does today. A real show will surface events I have not imagined, and the extension test in §02 is the mechanism for adding them — but a mechanism is not evidence that the set is right.
Volume Three ends here Every sound in this document is generated from its specification. There is no gap between the spec and the asset because there is no asset — which means this volume cannot rot the way a folder of WAV files does. One honest note on delivery: the voices here are rendered once through an offline audio context and played back, rather than streamed from a live one. The synthesis is identical sample for sample; only the delivery differs. The product uses a live context, because that is the only way to meet the sub-20 ms latency C27 requires — but a document embedded in a frame can be permitted an audio context and still be refused an output path, and shipping a sound specification that is silent for half its readers would be the worst possible way to make this argument.
