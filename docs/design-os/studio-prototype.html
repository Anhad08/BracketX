What this changed

edits, not additions

Building the prototype forced five decisions and exposed one contradiction. All of them are edits to existing volumes; no volume was added.

Kept
The component surface is the beginner interface. The Blueprint already defined a component's declared surface — the properties an instance may override. Making that the default panel required no new concept: a beginner sees Name, Role, Logo, Colour, Entrance because that is what the component promised, and an expert sees the layers underneath. One mechanism, two depths.
Kept
The beginner surface is also the on-air surface. The contradiction that solved itself. Volume Two forbids editing construction on air but content is an operator's job — which is exactly the beginner/expert split, arriving from the opposite direction. Go live in the prototype: the fields stay, the layers grey out. No rule was added; two existing rules turned out to be the same rule.
Edited
Blueprint §03 — the entrance vocabulary drops from seven presets to five. Type on and Count are not entrances; they are HOLD behaviours that happen to start at frame zero. Listing them beside Rise + fade made the beginner's first choice a seven-way decision where five of the options are wrong for their case. Moved to the HOLD vocabulary in Amendment 3, where they already belonged.
Edited
Volume One §06 — the empty state rule gains a clause. Law 7 says every surface answers “what now?”. The prototype's first screen showed that this is not enough for a first run: the honest answer to “what now?” on an empty Studio is a question, not a button. A first-run surface asks what the operator is making before it shows them anything. Three templates, no blank canvas, no tour.
Removed
The band buttons and frame counts leave the beginner transport entirely. IN, HOLD, OUT and the frame readout are mechanism. A first-time user needs Play, Cue and Take — and Take is in the same place it is in every other Streamatrix surface, because Volume Two H3 says the same action occupies the same pixels.
Edited
Volume Five's attention budget applies to teaching, too. The prototype shows one coached hint, once, at the moment it is relevant, dismissible for ever — not a tour and not a sequence. A five-step onboarding is five simultaneous demands on someone who has not yet made anything, which is the budget violated before the product has done anything useful.
The convergence test Every control in this prototype answers all four questions, or it is not here. Can an engineer build it? Every field maps to a document property that already exists in the scene format. Motion is { preset, ease, frames, stagger }; content is the component's override map; the confidence renders are additional projections of one document.
Can an operator understand it? Five fields, three buttons, and the two most consequential are in the position they occupy everywhere else in the product.
Can a broadcaster trust it? Take is instant, tally is unmistakable, construction locks on air, and the 9:16 overflow is caught while you type rather than after transmission.
Can a beginner use it? Type a name, pick a colour, press Take. Nothing above requires knowing what a keyframe is.

Still not done, and honestly stated: text editing on the canvas is unspecified and remains the largest gap — here the name is edited in a field, which is right for a beginner and insufficient for a designer. The prototype fakes the engine: the confidence renders are DOM, not projections. And the overflow check is a width comparison rather than the real shaping pass. Each of those is the same work an implementation would do properly, and none changes a decision above.
