Streamatrix StudioSpecification & Audit

The authoring environment

The Design OS decided how Streamatrix behaves. This decides what Studio is — every region, every control, every binding, every duration — to the level where an engineer can build it without asking a question.

It opens with the screen, because a specification that cannot be looked at is a specification nobody checks. Then 24 findings against the product as previously described, including three that block the build and one rule in the Design OS itself that makes Studio unusable as written.

03

Viewport

the heart

The viewport is not a canvas. A canvas is infinite and a broadcast frame is 1920 × 1080 exactly — modelling it as a canvas is a lie about the medium that produces every downstream error, from graphics placed outside the raster to designers who never learn where title safe is.

V-1 · the frame is the world The viewport shows a fixed raster at the production's delivery resolution. There is no space outside the frame to place things in — objects may extend past the edge, but nothing can be parked there. The pasteboard that every design tool provides is where forgotten objects go to be rendered accidentally at 20:00. Streamatrix has none. An object dragged fully outside the frame snaps back to the nearest edge with 25 % of its bounds inside, and the layer is flagged off frame.

04

Timeline

playable · rebuilt

The brief asked whether today's timeline is the best possible timeline for broadcast graphics. It is not, and the reason is structural rather than cosmetic: every timeline in this industry is borrowed from video editing, and a broadcast graphic is not a clip.

A clip has a duration. A lower third does not — it has an entrance, an indefinite hold that lasts exactly as long as the director wants, and an exit. Modelling that as a fixed-length strip forces every designer to invent a convention, and every facility invents a different one.

T-1 · three bands, not one duration A graphic's timeline is IN · HOLD · OUT. IN and OUT have fixed frame counts. HOLD is elastic and unbounded, and is the only band in which time does not advance during design. This is the single most consequential decision in Studio. It makes the elastic middle explicit instead of conventional, it makes OUT authorable without guessing where it starts, and it means a designer can never accidentally place a keyframe in a region whose duration is unknown. Loops, data reactions and idle motion live in HOLD; everything else cannot.
Lower Third · timelinedrag the playhead · toggle what you see
Unit is frames at delivery rate. Never seconds, never milliseconds. Sub-frame keyframes are impossible by construction.

Three inventions, and why each earns its place

T-2
The lane draws the value, not the keyframes. Every property lane plots its actual curve across the band. Easing becomes something you see rather than something you open a graph editor to inspect. Why it matters: the commonest animation defect in broadcast graphics is an ease that looks wrong at speed, and it is invisible in every diamond-based timeline until you play it back. Keyframes remain as markers on the curve; the curve is the primary object.
T-3
Timing is relative and named, not absolute. A layer starts +3f after Bar, not at frame 9. The tether is drawn. Moving Bar moves everything downstream and the stagger is preserved. Why it matters: broadcast graphics are almost entirely staggered reveals, and absolute timing means every timing change is a manual re-layout of the whole graphic. This converts the most common edit from twenty drags to one.
T-4
Easing is chosen from the Design OS curves, by name. Press, glide, weight — the same three the product itself moves with, plus linear for data-driven values. No bezier handles by default. Why it matters: a graphics package whose animations share the interface's physics feels like one object, and unrestricted beziers are how a facility ends up with forty subtly different eases nobody can name. Custom beziers exist behind ⌥ on the ease control for the case that genuinely needs one.

06

Inspector

fewest clicks

The property panel is where a designer spends most of their day and where most tools waste most of their time. Three decisions do the work.

I-1
Every numeric field is a scrubber, a stepper and a text field at once. Drag horizontally to scrub. ⌥ for 0.1×, ⇧ for 10×. Arrow keys step; with ⇧, ten. Click once to type; the value is selected on focus. Expressions are accepted — +12, *1.5, 1920/3. Why: these are three different jobs on one number and every tool that separates them costs a click on each.
I-2
The inspector shows what is set, not everything that exists. Bound, overridden and non-default properties are shown. Defaults collapse into a single Show all row per group. Why: a text layer has about 40 properties and a designer touches four. Scrolling past 36 defaults, forty times a day, is the tax.
I-3
Binding state is on the field, not in a separate panel. A bound field carries the accent border and shows the variable name and its live value. Clicking the name jumps to the variable. Why: this makes the inspector a debugger. "Why is this blank?" is answered where the question is asked, rather than three panels away.
Panel rules
RuleSpecification
Multi-selectShows the intersection of properties. Differing values read — and typing sets all. Never blanks the panel.
No selectionShows the graphic's own properties: size, delivery resolution, background, band durations. Never an empty panel — Law 7.

08

Keyboard

62 bindings · no collisions

Studio's map, complete. It rests on finding F-01 — that Volume Two's rule K1 ("unmodified keys belong to live operation; authoring always takes a modifier") makes a professional authoring tool impossible, because tool selection by single letter is the deepest muscle memory in this profession and putting it behind a modifier costs a keystroke on every single action of the day.

The narrowest correction is the one K2 already permits: a binding may change meaning across the on-air boundary. So single letters are tools while the production is off air, and become inert while it is on air — where the live transport set takes over. Nothing else changes, and the original safety property is preserved exactly: a stray keypress can never edit the document during a show.

Bindings not printed on a control7 — the viewport overlays. They live in the View menu and the palette; there is no persistent control to print them on. Accepted, noted.

Honest Limitations

what this spec does not solve
L1
The three-band timeline cannot express a graphic with two exits. A scoreboard that leaves differently on a goal than at half time has one OUT. The honest answer today is two graphics; the dishonest one is a state machine bolted onto the bands. I have chosen the constraint, and it will be wrong for some real graphics.
L2
Tethers can form cycles and this spec does not say what happens. A after B, B after A. Detection is easy; the interface for resolving it is not designed, and I would rather state that than draw a dialog I have not thought through.
L3
No 3D, and the viewport spec assumes there never will be. Camera presets, zoom steps and the fixed raster are all 2D concepts. When virtual sets arrive this section needs rewriting rather than extending, and pretending otherwise now would produce a worse 2D tool.
L4
Text editing is specified thinly. Enter, edit, exit — but not selection semantics, not bidirectional text, not vertical scripts, not ligature-aware caret movement. This is a genuine gap and it is a week of work by someone who knows typography systems.
L5
The workflow action counts are estimates. They are derived from the specification, not measured with designers. They are stated as acceptance criteria because that makes them falsifiable — but nobody has yet failed to hit them, because nobody has tried.
L6
Nothing here has been built. The standing limitation across every document in this project. This is a specification of an interface that does not exist, written by someone who has not watched anyone use it.
14

Readiness

the score

Scored against one question: could a competent engineering team build this without asking a question that changes the design? Not "is it good" — buildable without guessing.

By areaspecification completeness, not quality
Weighted by how much of a designer's day the area occupies, not by how much of this document it takes up.
Final readiness · 82 / 100 Studio is specified to the point where an engineering team could build it, and would need to make roughly a dozen decisions of their own — all of them local, none of them architectural. What is genuinely finished: the layout, the viewport, the timeline model, the keyboard, the pointer, the motion inventory and the performance budgets. An engineer opening any of those sections gets a value, not an intention.

What is not: text editing (L4) is the one real hole and it is in the most-used tool in the product. Asset browser and theme editor are specified only where they touch Studio. And every number in §11 is an estimate presented as a criterion.

The score is not higher because of L6, and it cannot be. A specification that has never met a designer is a hypothesis with good handwriting. 82 is what I believe a document like this is worth before anyone has tried to build from it — and the fastest way to move it is not another pass, it is one engineer implementing §04 and telling me every place they had to guess.
