What you are actually testing

Interruptibility

Throw the dock closed, then catch it before it lands. It follows your pointer from wherever it is — it does not finish closing first. Every animation starts from the live on-screen value.

Velocity handoff

The spring continues at the exact speed your pointer was moving, so there is no seam between dragging and animating. A slow drag past halfway still closes; a fast flick back does not.

Momentum projection

Release decides by where the gesture was going, not where it stopped — the same exponential decay a scroll uses. A small flick makes a big movement.

Rubber-banding

Push the dock past its open position. It resists progressively rather than stopping dead, so the boundary reads as responsive rather than frozen.

The objection this page exists to test

I raised a risk when I proposed this direction: broadcast graphics are transparent, and Apple's own rule is never to stack a light translucent surface on another. Press “Slide the graphic under the dock” and judge it yourself — that is the failure case, not a hypothetical.

Press “Solid chrome” to see the same layout with the blur removed. If you cannot tell much difference, that is an argument the translucency is costing frame budget for very little — and this product already runs four live WebGL contexts.

My read: glass is right for the rail and toolbars, which sit over chrome, and wrong for the dock, which sits over the work. A panel you read numbers in wants a solid ground.

If you pick this

It is the largest of the three builds. Springs, pointer capture, velocity tracking and projection are engineering rather than CSS — roughly what you have just used here, applied to every panel, sheet and drawer in the product. The payoff is that the interface stops feeling like a form and starts feeling like a thing.
